The Flop the world arrives

Before you can act, you have to model the world.

Poker makes this problem obvious. You see two cards, then slowly three more, four, five — but what you are actually trying to know is everything you cannot see: another person’s range, their beliefs about your range, the shape of the hand several streets ahead. Every bet is an observation; every observation is ambiguous. You build a distribution, update it, commit chips to it. The game is not a game of cards. It is an inference problem wearing a costume.

Von Neumann understood this. He founded modern game theory less with chess, where everything is visible, than with poker, where almost nothing is. Out of that came minimax, decision theory, and most of the mathematical machinery we now point at artificial intelligence. Maria Konnikova, coming the other way — from cognitive science into the tour — has written the same idea as a psychologist: the felt is a laboratory for how humans reason under uncertainty. Both arrive at the same claim: intelligence is, at bottom, the ability to model a world you cannot fully see.

The philosophers got here first, without chips. Aristotle’s phantasia — retained, manipulable appearances — already pushes against the idea that cognition is only immediate perception: the mind holds the world even when the world is absent. Helmholtz sharpens it: perception is unconscious inference, the brain reconstructing distal structure from impoverished sensory stimulation. Wilfrid Sellars, most uncompromising of the three, attacks the Myth of the Given — the temptation to treat raw sensation as a self-sufficient foundation for knowledge. A sensory stream is not yet an intelligible world. Knowledge requires concepts, relations, inference, explanatory integration. Sellars draws a line between the manifest image — the world as lived, with objects and persons and intentions — and the scientific image — the world as hidden causes and dynamical laws. Intelligence lives at that seam: between appearance and explanation, between being embedded in the world and modeling it from within.

This is also what embodied AI is trying to do. An agent does not receive a finished world. It receives a stream of pixels, proprioception, forces — a partial, noisy, sequential observation process — and must somehow recover enough latent structure to simulate, plan, and act. The technical questions I work on — representation learning, generative modeling, multimodality, world models, physical reasoning — are the same family of questions a good player is asking at the table, scaled up and written in code. How do you infer what you cannot see? How much do you trust your model before acting on it? When do you commit the stack?

Poker is what happens when those questions get a felt, a deck, and a clock. Research is what happens when they get compute.
